# Qin Unification of China

> Qin won interstate competition through unusually deep mobilization and administration, then demonstrated that coercive capacity can unify territory faster than it can create durable legitimacy.

## Causal stack

### Structural pressures
- Centuries of Warring States competition rewarded taxation, census, military mobilization, and bureaucratic reform.
- Qin had earlier Legalist reforms that weakened hereditary aristocracy and strengthened direct state control.

### Enabling conditions
- Agricultural expansion, standardized ranks, roads, and military organization supported large armies.
- Rival states were defeated sequentially rather than as one unified coalition.

## Turning points
- Conquests from 230 to 221 BCE ended the rival kingdoms.
- Commanderies/counties and standardization extended central rule over conquered space.
- After the First Emperor's death, succession manipulation and rebellion exposed regime brittleness.

## Why it changed the system
- The Qin dynasty fell quickly, but many administrative and standardization practices survived into the Han.
- Imperial unification became a powerful recurring Chinese political ideal.

## Competing interpretation

Qin is both a success in state capacity and a warning against equating extraction with legitimacy.

## Counterfactual

A less coercive post-conquest consolidation and more stable succession may have prolonged Qin rule without changing the broad move toward centralized empire.
