# Rise and Breakup of Standard Oil

> Standard Oil combined genuine efficiency and coordination gains with bargaining power and exclusionary conduct; its scale helped create the political and legal institutions that later constrained monopoly.

## Causal stack

### Structural pressures
- Early oil refining was fragmented, volatile, and dependent on rail transport.
- Scale created potential savings in refining, shipping, byproduct use, and distribution.

### Enabling conditions
- Corporate/legal innovations allowed coordination across many entities.
- Railroads competed for large-volume traffic, giving major refiners bargaining leverage.

## Turning points
- The Cleveland consolidation rapidly expanded Standard Oil's refining position.
- Pipelines reduced transport dependence and extended vertical control.
- Trust structures centralized control while avoiding some state corporate limits.
- The 1911 Supreme Court decision broke Standard Oil into multiple companies.

## Why it changed the system
- Several successor firms remained large and valuable, showing that breakup did not erase operational assets.
- US antitrust policy became a permanent constraint on corporate strategy.

## Competing interpretation

A pure 'robber baron' story misses efficiency; a pure efficiency story misses coercive market power and political legitimacy.

## Counterfactual

A more open contracting strategy might preserve some integration benefits while reducing the political coalition for breakup.
