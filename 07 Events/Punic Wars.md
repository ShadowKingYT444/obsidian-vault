# Punic Wars

> Rome defeated Carthage not because it avoided catastrophic losses but because its alliance network, manpower mobilization, fiscal persistence, and strategic adaptation survived them.

## Causal stack

### Structural pressures
- Rome and Carthage were expanding powers with overlapping interests in Sicily and the western Mediterranean.
- Rome's Italian alliance system gave it exceptional replacement capacity; Carthage relied more heavily on overseas possessions and varied troop sources.

### Enabling conditions
- Rome learned naval warfare in the First Punic War and could replace fleets after disasters.
- Carthaginian expansion in Iberia created resources and a base for Hannibal.

## Turning points
- Hannibal's victories at Trebia, Trasimene, and Cannae did not break enough Roman allies away.
- Rome shifted toward containment in Italy while attacking Carthaginian resource bases in Iberia.
- Scipio's invasion of Africa forced Hannibal home; Zama ended the Second Punic War.

## Why it changed the system
- Rome became the dominant western Mediterranean power.
- The destruction of Carthage in 146 BCE symbolized a later phase of Roman expansion, not an inevitable result of Cannae.

## Competing interpretation

Tactical histories emphasize Hannibal and Scipio; strategic histories stress institutions, coalition resilience, manpower, finance, and theater selection.

## Counterfactual

If a larger share of Rome's Italian allies defects after Cannae, Hannibal's tactical success has a political mechanism for becoming strategic victory.
