# Global Financial Crisis of 2008

> A housing downturn became a systemic crisis because leverage, opaque securitization, short-term funding, weak underwriting, interconnected balance sheets, and fragile confidence amplified losses.

## Causal stack

### Structural pressures
- Low perceived risk and abundant global savings supported credit growth.
- US housing finance mixed private securitization, government policy, rating agencies, and shadow-banking structures.

### Enabling conditions
- Institutions funded long-duration risky assets with runnable short-term liabilities.
- Complex securities made exposure difficult to map quickly during stress.

## Turning points
- Subprime delinquencies rose and structured-credit valuations deteriorated.
- Bear Stearns failed in early 2008; Lehman's bankruptcy in September triggered broad funding panic.
- Governments and central banks used liquidity, guarantees, recapitalization, and unconventional monetary policy to prevent deeper collapse.

## Why it changed the system
- A severe recession and unemployment shock followed; public debt and political distrust increased.
- Financial regulation changed, but debates over moral hazard and too-big-to-fail remain.

## Competing interpretation

Blaming only borrowers, banks, regulators, monetary policy, or government housing policy misses the interacting leverage/funding/network system.

## Counterfactual

A managed Lehman resolution with credible system-wide guarantees might reduce panic, but underlying housing losses and leverage would still require recapitalization.
