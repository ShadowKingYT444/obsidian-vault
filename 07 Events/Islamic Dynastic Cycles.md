# Islamic Dynastic Cycles

> Ibn Khaldun used repeated dynastic turnover in North Africa and the wider Islamic world to ask a general question: why can cohesive frontier groups conquer richer states, then lose the cohesion that helped them win?

## Causal stack

### Structural pressures

- Political authority often depended on bargaining with tribes, military households, cities, tax intermediaries, and religious elites.
- Large territories created high communication and enforcement costs.
- Dynasties needed revenue from settled populations while often recruiting military power from groups outside the urban core.

### Enabling conditions

- Strong kinship or coalition solidarity could lower coordination cost inside a rising group.
- Weak or divided incumbent regimes created openings for outsiders with higher military cohesion.

## Turning points

- A cohesive group acquires enough military and political strength to seize a state.
- The new dynasty uses taxation, administration, and urban institutions to consolidate rule.
- Court life, factional competition, fiscal demands, and reliance on professional forces can weaken the original coalition.
- A new peripheral coalition can then challenge the center.

## Why it changed the system

This is less one bounded event than a repeated historical pattern that informed Ibn Khaldun's theory of `asabiyyah`. It matters because it is an early attempt to explain political change through social mechanisms rather than lists of rulers.

## Competing interpretation

The cycle is not a universal law. Geography, institutions, technology, religion, trade, and external enemies vary too much for every dynasty to follow one sequence.

## Counterfactual

A dynasty that converts early coalition cohesion into durable, impersonal institutions can reduce dependence on the founder group and break part of the predicted cycle.
