# Meiji Restoration

> Japan's transformation after Western coercive entry succeeded through selective institutional copying, centralized fiscal-military reform, mass education, and domestic political struggle—not by copying 'the West' wholesale.

## Causal stack

### Structural pressures
- Tokugawa domains had high literacy, commercial networks, and decentralized administrative capacity.
- Western naval power exposed the military vulnerability of the old diplomatic order.

### Enabling conditions
- Reform coalitions in Satsuma, Choshu, and other domains had military and administrative experience.
- Leaders could inspect foreign institutions directly through missions and foreign advisers.

## Turning points
- Perry's arrival intensified legitimacy crisis around the shogunate.
- The 1868 restoration shifted formal authority to the imperial state.
- Abolition of domains, conscription, tax reform, education, and new ministries increased central capacity.
- The 1889 constitution institutionalized a new political order without full liberal democracy.

## Why it changed the system
- Japan industrialized and became a major military power.
- State-building later supported imperial expansion and colonial violence across Asia.

## Competing interpretation

The 'miracle of imitation' story underweights Tokugawa prerequisites and domestic conflict over which foreign models to adopt.

## Counterfactual

Without preexisting literacy, markets, and capable domains, imported institutions would have much weaker execution capacity.
