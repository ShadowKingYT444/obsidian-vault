# Chinese Civil War and 1949 Revolution

> Communist victory resulted from a long interaction of rural organization, war against Japan, Nationalist institutional weaknesses, military adaptation, land politics, and changing legitimacy—not a single decisive campaign.

## Causal stack

### Structural pressures
- The Republic of China faced warlord fragmentation, limited fiscal reach, rural inequality, and foreign invasion.
- Nationalists and Communists competed to define national unification and social reform.

### Enabling conditions
- The Japanese invasion weakened Nationalist capacity and changed territorial/political opportunities.
- Communist rural bases created local organization, recruitment, and taxation mechanisms.

## Turning points
- The Long March preserved a Communist core after severe defeat.
- The anti-Japanese war expanded Communist influence in parts of rural China.
- Post-1945 civil war shifted as Nationalist inflation, morale, and governance deteriorated while Communist forces gained conventional capability.

## Why it changed the system
- The People's Republic of China was founded in 1949; the Republic of China government retreated to Taiwan.
- Land reform and later socialist transformation radically changed property and social institutions, often through severe coercion.

## Competing interpretation

Accounts differ over military competence, peasant support, coercion, Soviet/US effects, Japanese invasion, and Nationalist governance; outcome cannot be explained by ideology alone.

## Counterfactual

Without the Japanese invasion, Communist survival and expansion face a much harder path, but Nationalist structural problems do not disappear.
