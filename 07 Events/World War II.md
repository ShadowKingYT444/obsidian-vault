# World War II

> Axis expansion initially exploited operational surprise and opponents' political weakness, but the war became a contest of industrial capacity, coalition coordination, logistics, technology, and mass mobilization alongside genocidal ideology.

## Causal stack

### Structural pressures
- The post–World War I order was politically fragile and the Great Depression weakened democracies and international cooperation.
- Nazi Germany, Fascist Italy, and imperial Japan pursued revisionist expansion with different goals.

### Enabling conditions
- Mechanized forces, radio, air power, and operational doctrine enabled rapid early campaigns.
- Industrial economies could mobilize production at scales far beyond earlier wars.

## Turning points
- Germany's invasion of the Soviet Union opened a vast attritional front.
- Japan's attack on Pearl Harbor brought US industrial power formally into war.
- Midway, El Alamein, Stalingrad, and later Allied strategic offensives shifted initiative.
- Normandy and Soviet offensives compressed Germany from west and east; atomic bombs and Soviet entry against Japan preceded Japanese surrender.

## Why it changed the system
- Tens of millions died, including six million Jews murdered in the Holocaust and millions of other victims of Nazi mass murder.
- The US and USSR emerged as superpowers; decolonization accelerated and new international institutions were built.

## Competing interpretation

Leader decisions matter, but industrial output, geography, logistics, ideology, occupation policy, resistance, and coalition learning explain why early operational success did not equal strategic victory.

## Counterfactual

Axis victory becomes more plausible only under multiple changed conditions; avoiding a two-front war alone does not solve long-run US/Soviet industrial imbalance.
