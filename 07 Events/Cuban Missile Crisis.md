# Cuban Missile Crisis

> The crisis shows that escalation control depends on incomplete information, organizational procedures, private bargaining, and leaders preserving exits for opponents—not simply on 'standing firm.'

## Causal stack

### Structural pressures
- US nuclear forces threatened the USSR and US Jupiter missiles were deployed in Turkey.
- After the Bay of Pigs, Cuba expected further US pressure and sought stronger deterrence.

### Enabling conditions
- Soviet leaders attempted secret missile deployment in Cuba.
- Reconnaissance and intelligence created a narrow period in which US leaders knew enough to respond but not everything about Soviet/Cuban capabilities.

## Turning points
- Kennedy rejected immediate air-strike/invasion options and used a naval quarantine.
- A Soviet missile shot down a U-2 over Cuba, while another U-2 strayed near Soviet airspace, increasing accidental-escalation risk.
- Public and private messages produced a deal: Soviet missiles left Cuba; the US publicly pledged not to invade and privately agreed to remove Jupiter missiles from Turkey later.

## Why it changed the system
- Both sides improved crisis communication, including the hotline, and became more alert to nuclear escalation risk.
- The popular memory of unilateral coercive victory obscures reciprocal concessions.

## Competing interpretation

Rational-actor stories miss organizational accidents, unauthorized risks, and uncertainty documented later.

## Counterfactual

An immediate US air strike might miss some missiles and trigger Soviet/Cuban retaliation, creating a much more dangerous escalation ladder.
