# Manhattan Project and Early Computing

> Wartime problems concentrated money, talent, secrecy, instrumentation, and management at unprecedented scale; computing advanced because difficult physics and cryptanalysis created demand for faster calculation and programmable machines.

## Causal stack

### Structural pressures
- Total war created urgent calculation problems in ballistics, cryptanalysis, nuclear physics, and logistics.
- Universities, industry, and government had growing electrical and mathematical expertise.

### Enabling conditions
- Federal funding could support large teams and custom equipment without normal commercial payback schedules.
- Vacuum tubes, relays, punch cards, and mathematical methods were improving simultaneously.

## Turning points
- Bletchley machines and US projects accelerated specialized computation.
- Los Alamos required large numerical calculations for weapon design.
- ENIAC and EDVAC-era work pushed electronic and stored-program architectures forward.

## Why it changed the system
- Postwar laboratories, defense funding, and computing firms inherited people and techniques.
- The same institutional model raised enduring questions about secrecy, military patronage, and scientist responsibility.

## Competing interpretation

The computer was not invented by one person or one war project; wartime funding accelerated convergence among many prewar ideas and teams.

## Counterfactual

Without wartime demand, electronic computing likely still develops, but capital-intensive experimentation and deployment proceed more slowly.
