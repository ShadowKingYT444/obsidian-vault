# Scientific Revolution

> Modern science emerged from a changing system of instruments, mathematics, print, correspondence, institutions, craft knowledge, and arguments about evidence—not from a single 'scientific method' appearing at once.

## Causal stack

### Structural pressures
- Universities preserved scholastic traditions while navigation, artillery, mining, and commerce created practical measurement problems.
- Print allowed faster comparison of observations and diagrams.

### Enabling conditions
- Telescopes, microscopes, clocks, air pumps, and precision instruments created new observable phenomena.
- Scientific societies and correspondence networks made public replication more valuable.

## Turning points
- Copernicus reorganized planetary models around heliocentrism.
- Kepler derived mathematical planetary laws; Galileo used telescopic and mechanical evidence.
- Newton's Principia unified terrestrial and celestial mechanics.

## Why it changed the system
- Mathematical prediction and experimental replication gained institutional authority.
- New models altered navigation, engineering, astronomy, and later industrial science.

## Competing interpretation

The label risks making a gradual, uneven, multinational process look like one coherent revolution; medieval and Islamic scientific continuities matter.

## Counterfactual

Without print and instrument-making networks, mathematical ideas could still advance but verification and diffusion would slow sharply.
