# Reconstruction

> Reconstruction attempted to build biracial democracy after slavery, but federal commitment and enforcement proved insufficient against organized white-supremacist violence and shifting northern political incentives.

## Causal stack

### Structural pressures
- Emancipation destroyed slavery but left land ownership and local power highly unequal.
- Former Confederate states had to be reintegrated while defining Black citizenship and voting rights.

### Enabling conditions
- The 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendments created constitutional tools for national civil-rights enforcement.
- Black political organization and Union occupation made new governments possible.

## Turning points
- Congressional Reconstruction imposed stronger conditions after conflict with Andrew Johnson.
- Freedmen's Bureau and federal enforcement supported schools, labor claims, and political rights with limited resources.
- Ku Klux Klan and other violent networks attacked Black voters and Republican governments.
- The 1877 political settlement coincided with withdrawal of remaining federal troops and declining enforcement.

## Why it changed the system
- Constitutional amendments remained a future legal foundation for civil rights.
- Jim Crow regimes later destroyed much of Reconstruction's political gains.

## Competing interpretation

Calling Reconstruction simply a corrupt failure reproduces older racist historiography; the deeper issue is why democratic institution-building was not defended against organized violence.

## Counterfactual

Sustained federal enforcement and stronger economic redistribution could change the durability of Black political power, though northern willingness was uncertain.
