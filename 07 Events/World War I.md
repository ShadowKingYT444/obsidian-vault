# World War I

> A regional assassination became a world war because alliance commitments, mobilization plans, security fears, imperial rivalry, and leaders' choices interacted inside a system where delay itself seemed dangerous.

## Causal stack

### Structural pressures
- European great powers were tied through alliance blocs, arms races, and repeated Balkan crises.
- Industrial mobilization plans were large, rigid, and time-sensitive, making partial moves hard to reverse.

### Enabling conditions
- Railways enabled rapid mass mobilization while telegraphy accelerated diplomatic and military signaling.
- Nationalism and imperial competition increased the political cost of backing down.

## Turning points
- The July Crisis converted the Sarajevo assassination into sequential mobilizations and declarations.
- The western front stabilized into attritional trench warfare.
- US entry and the failure of Germany's 1918 offensives shifted the final balance as Central Power resources collapsed.

## Why it changed the system
- Four empires fell; borders and states were remade across Europe and the Middle East.
- Mass death, debt, revolution, and punitive/unstable peace politics shaped the interwar crisis.

## Competing interpretation

No single cause—alliances, Germany, nationalism, mobilization, or accident—is sufficient; the key problem is how system structure and agency combined during July 1914.

## Counterfactual

A localized Austro-Serbian conflict required other powers to accept temporary strategic losses rather than activate full mobilization; politically possible, but leaders judged the risks differently.
