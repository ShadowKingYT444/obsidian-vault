# Green Revolution

> Higher-yield crop varieties mattered because they arrived with irrigation, fertilizer, credit, extension, transport, and policy; the 'seed' was only one component of an agricultural production system.

## Causal stack

### Structural pressures
- Rapid population growth increased food-security pressure in many countries.
- Plant breeding and agricultural research institutions had become more capable of controlled selection.

### Enabling conditions
- Synthetic fertilizer, irrigation, pesticides, rural credit, and extension services could complement new varieties.
- Governments and foundations financed research centers and distribution.

## Turning points
- Semi-dwarf wheat work in Mexico improved yield response to fertilizer.
- India and Pakistan adopted high-yield wheat and rice packages during the 1960s.
- Expansion reduced dependence on some grain imports but varied strongly by region and farm access.

## Why it changed the system
- Cereal yields rose substantially in several regions and famine risk changed.
- Benefits were uneven; water use, fertilizer runoff, biodiversity loss, and unequal access created new costs.

## Competing interpretation

The Green Revolution was not one invention and did not 'solve hunger'; hunger also depends on income, distribution, conflict, and institutions.

## Counterfactual

Distributing improved seed without irrigation, fertilizer, credit, and market access yields much smaller gains.
