# Napoleonic Wars

> Napoleon's system repeatedly created local superiority through tempo and corps organization, but opposing states learned, mobilized deeper resources, and eventually exploited French overextension in Iberia and Russia.

## Causal stack

### Structural pressures
- The French Revolution had expanded mobilization and weakened old regimes' military assumptions.
- Britain's sea power and finance made it hard for France to convert continental victories into total strategic victory.

### Enabling conditions
- French corps organization supported independent marches and rapid concentration.
- Napoleon controlled a large administrative-military information network and a battle-experienced officer corps.

## Turning points
- Ulm-Austerlitz in 1805 demonstrated the high-tempo system at its peak.
- The Peninsular War created sustained attrition and political resistance.
- The 1812 invasion of Russia destroyed much of the Grande Armée.
- The 1813–14 coalition coordinated more effectively and avoided giving Napoleon easy isolated victories; Waterloo ended the Hundred Days.

## Why it changed the system
- European borders and institutions were reworked; Napoleonic legal/administrative reforms survived in many areas.
- Coalition powers designed a postwar settlement aimed at preventing renewed continental hegemony.

## Competing interpretation

Napoleon was not simply defeated by winter; political aims, distance, supply, attrition, Russian strategy, coalition learning, and French commitments interacted.

## Counterfactual

If Napoleon accepts a bounded settlement after major victories, the French imperial system may persist longer; the central problem is whether he could credibly stop revising the order.
