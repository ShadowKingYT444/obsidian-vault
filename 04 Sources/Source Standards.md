# Source Standards

Historical notes in this vault are study maps, not final authority.

## Evidence ladder

1. **Primary evidence:** letters, speeches, laws, diaries, official records, artifacts, contemporary data.
2. **Critical editions / scholarly translations:** primary material with provenance and context.
3. **Modern scholarly synthesis:** historians who compare sources and address historiography.
4. **Serious biography / narrative history:** useful for sequence and character, but still interpretive.
5. **Popular media:** good for discovery, weak as the only support for strong claims.

## Claim discipline

- Separate what happened from why it happened.
- Mark disputed numbers and quotations.
- Do not infer a private motive from a later outcome without evidence.
- Do not treat a vivid anecdote as a representative sample.
- Prefer multiple independent sources for extraordinary claims.
- Record when a source has an agenda, retrospective bias, access problem, or translation problem.

## Biography-specific warning

Famous people generate both hostile and admiring myth. Success also creates survivorship bias: the archive contains more stories about the winner. Always compare the person with rivals, institutions, and base rates.

If a note contains a lesson you want to use in your own life, raise the evidence standard rather than lowering it.
